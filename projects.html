Constraining the Rates, Controls, and Spatial Distributions of Landslides in Southern Calabria, Italy

Calabria is recognised as one of the most seismically active and fastest uplifting regions in the Mediterranean, where landslides are the common hillslope mass-wasting process. This study presents a new multi-decadal landslide inventory for Reggio Calabria province in southern Calabria covering a 24-year period from 2000 to 2024 at 6-year intervals. The inventory includes ~1700 landslides mapped using Google Earth and WMS orthophotos, which reveal that sediment fluxes are characterised by smaller, less frequent landslides, following a power-law distribution (β = 1.7 ± 0.01). The estimation of erosion rates requires an understanding of landslides size. Here, a regionally-calibrated area-volume scaling relationship from published data is employed, translating to rates of landslide-derived erosion between 0.02¬ and 0.1 ± 0.03 mm/yr. Topographic and lithological controls on landslide occurrence are investigated using slope aspect and angle, along with four main lithological units to quantify enhancement factors and frequency-density distributions. Results establish south facing slopes as facilitating larger landslide magnitudes, despite demonstrating higher Normalised Difference Vegetation Index values than north facing slopes. Peak landslides activity is concentrated on slopes between 50° and 65°, comprising mostly metamorphic lithology. Landsliding also favours both carbonate and metamorphic units, with frequency-density distributions establishing power-law exponents of β = 1.8 ± 0.02 and β = 2.5 ± 0.02, respectively. Lastly, Google Earth Engine was used to compute and align annual mean precipitation with each temporal interval. Results highlight an inconclusive statistical relationship suggesting that precipitation alone cannot fully explain variations in landslide-derived erosion.

Landslide Frequency and Magnitude in the Southern Alps, New Zealand

Mountain regions are shaped by complicated interactions between tectonics and climatic forces. Understanding the orientation of the processes influencing landslide magnitude and frequency is an important part of geomorphological research and hazard mitigation. The Southern Alps are formed by dextral transpression at the Alpine Fault, where oblique convergence between the Pacific and Australian plate result in horizontal and vertical strike-slip displacement. Consequently, the orogen receives significant uplift rates, along with abundant daily and annual rainfall. This study uses Google Earth Pro and historical aerial imagery to identify and map landslides over a 70-year period. Volume-area scaling relationships establish landslide-derived erosion rates in the range 0.09–0.51 mm/yr, with proximal activity concentrated within 5–10 km of the Alpine Fault. Landslides display a power-law magnitude-frequency distribution in the range 100–10-5 km-2 yr-1. Power-law regressions yield a lower scaling coefficient compared to previous data, reflecting the shift to shallower landslides and the accuracy of Google Earth Pro implementation. Strong positive correlations are found between landslide-derived erosion rates and Late Quaternary throw rates (R² = 0.974, r = 0.987), indicating the first-order control of tectonics on mass wasting. Analysis of rainfall data reveals that intense, short-duration events are significant triggers for landslides, and a decrease in median landslide area since the 1960s further supports the transition from a system dominated by large, infrequent landsliding events to one characterised by smaller, more frequent events. The data reveals the critical influence of Southern Alps tectonics in setting the stage for climatically driven landsliding and the implications for understanding sediment transport and connectivity in long-term landscape evolution.
